# MINGUS I: ELEVATED RETURNS
*A Mingus Empire adventure — first in a series*
## Full Three-Act Structure

*"You are likely to be poked by a verdling — and then eaten, if you don't move."*

---

## DESIGN PHILOSOPHY

The Mingus range is a run of red-rock peaks, mesas and ridgelines above a high-desert valley. Somebody has built an empire on top of it and into it: cliff palaces, switchback stairs, gondolas, wind-scoured terraces, observatories, and mine shafts bored deep into the rock. It is called **the Mingus Empire**. It is brand new. It is bigger, louder and higher than anything nearby, it is gilded on surfaces that do not need gilding, and all of it can be seen from miles away. That last part is the point.

The **Verdance** runs along the valley floor. Past it lies the far bank, low and lush and green, and somewhere beneath that green is an older empire. It is underground, quiet and legendary, and it was there first. Nobody in the Mingus Empire has ever seen it. Everybody in the Mingus Empire talks about it.

The empire was founded eighteen months ago by **Lord Maximus Vaunt the First** (self-numbered; he plans to be several): heir to a parking-lot fortune, real estate visionary, serial bankrupt, blockchain evangelist. He launched **$ALT**, an "altitude-backed stablecoin" pegged to the sunrise and promising returns of 4000% APY ("elevated returns"). **Lady Esmé Vaunt**, in a spite-filing divorce maneuver, counter-launched **$SHRUG** and an NFT collection of her coat in various poses.

The empire's economy has collapsed into speculation. Murklings mine crypto in the only dark the mountain has. The parser charges gas fees. And on the highest mesa, Maximus Vaunt's greatest monument sits abandoned, its ballroom echoing with the ghosts of launch parties past. The ballroom is a replica of the famous hall in the old side, built from hearsay and built *upside down*.

**Tone**: The dry absurdism of the 1980s text adventure meets 2025's crypto winter. The game mocks pump-and-dump culture, VC mysticism, "community" as marketing, and the player's hope that this time the fundamentals are real. Underneath all of it runs one long joke: a sky-high newcomer that cannot stop looking down at a neighbor who never looks up.

**Core Mechanic**: Gas fees and token economics. Every meaningful action costs gas (volatile; price changes every 10 moves). Some "items" are NFTs—you own the receipt, not the thing. Two competing coins unlock different paths. The seed phrase is scattered across all three acts.

## THE RIVALRY

Think of two truck stops facing each other across a highway. There is the original, and then there is the newcomer. The newcomer's staff cannot stop talking about "that place across the street." The original's staff will not talk about the newcomer at all.

The game takes place entirely in the newcomer. **The old empire is never named, never mapped, and never visited.** Mingus people **always** call it **"the old side"** — or, pointing with their chin, **"across there."** Never a name, never a nickname that would admit it has one — just *the old side*, said the way you'd say "the competition." (Its people, when Mingus staff can't avoid mentioning them, are "old-siders.")

**The great river.** Everybody on the Mingus side is acutely aware that the Verdance **cannot be crossed** — it is the edge of their world, the thing every view from every terrace ends at. And the river has a second rule, said more quietly: **no one who has floated down it has ever come back.** There are names on a memorial at the bottom gondola station; nobody knows how far they got. *(This is the trilogy's spine: Mingus II is the first trip down the river by someone the player cares about; Mingus III is the first time anyone comes back.)* The two empires have opposite strengths, and the rivalry runs on them.

**The half-bridges.** The Mingans have tried, many times, to bridge the Verdance. **Every bridge has failed midway.** Not one has ever been completed. The valley is lined with them — stone arches, steel trusses, a gilded suspension span with Vaunt's name on both towers — each one marching confidently out over the water and then simply *stopping*, its last girder hanging over the current like a sentence nobody could finish. Each was a fully funded project with a whitepaper, a token and a roadmap; every roadmap ends at "Phase 2: Far Bank."

**Why they fail:** a bridge needs **pylons**, and pylons have to be sunk into the riverbed — which means **divers**. Nobody will dive the Verdance. The river is full of **awesomely huge creatures** that live in the deep green water (unnamed here; the river poets give them a dozen names and agree on none). Every bridge got as far as the last pylon anyone could sink from the shallows, and then the money, the divers and the nerve ran out at the same moment. Recruitment posters for "Verdance Foundation Divers — generous token allocation, vesting schedule TBD" still flap on the gondola platforms. There have been no applicants.

**The legend of the lantern.** Once — the Mingans swear it — a bridge got *close*. Closer than any before or since: the last span reached so far out that the engineers on the leading girder could see the far bank's reeds. And there, on the old side, stood **a figure holding up a shiny lantern** — it caught the light all the way across the water — staring straight at them, shouting. The wind and the river took the words. The engineers couldn't make them out. They decided — because they were Mingans, and the roadmap said Phase 2 — that the figure was **beckoning them on**. So they pushed forward. And the bridge, like every Mingan bridge, began to crack, and crumple, and fold, and went down into the Verdance with everything on it. The lantern stayed on the far bank a long while, people say, and then went out.
That's the legend, anyway. Nobody alive was there. The river poets sing it more than any other song, and they never agree on the one thing that matters: **whether the figure was calling them over, or telling them to stay away.** *(For the builder: the lantern is "shiny" — never "brass," never described further, nothing that ties it to any existing game; the figure is never identified. It's the series' most quoted story, and Mingus III can, very quietly, answer the question.)*

**The engineering grail:** so the great dream of Mingan engineering is **a bridge to the old side with no pylons at all**. Every guild, DAO and visionary has a scheme: a single cantilever counterweighted by the whole mountain; a suspension span hung from the summit and lowered like a fishing line; a "Bridge-as-a-Service" of linked balloons; a cable fired across by catapult (it fell short, and something large took it); a bridge made of verified NFT deeds laid end to end (it exists only on-chain). None has worked. Vaunt has announced three of them. Proving a pylon-less crossing is possible is the kind of thing a Mingan would sell their seed phrase for — and a puzzle seed for later games.

**The current** is the other half of the dread. Under the calm green surface the Verdance runs like a great continental river — broad, brown-shouldered, and *fast*: anything that goes in is gone downstream before it can shout. **Mingans have a shared nightmare**: climbing out onto an old half-bridge, the planks tilting, the slow slide to the broken end, and the drop — and then the current taking you, quick and quiet, down the river no one comes back from. Children dare each other to touch the first pylon. Nobody goes past the second.

**In play** *(hooks)*:
- **Mingus I:** the half-bridges are in view from every terrace — the empire's most honest monument. A seed-word or a song can sit on a bridge plaque ("Phase 2: Far Bank").
- **Mingus II:** the bridges are landmarks and hazards from the water — shadows overhead, debris below, a current that speeds up at every broken end. One of them might be the only place a boat can be stopped… or the place the player is *dropped* into the river in the first place.

**The funeral ships.** The Verdance is where Mingus sends its dead. When someone of standing dies, the **Mingans** build a ship and deck it out like a launch party: hard wallets strung like lanterns, $ALT medallions heaped in the hold, a gilded figurehead with laser eyes, the deceased's seed phrase engraved on a plaque and bolted to the mast. Then they send it down the river — **no one who has floated down it has ever come back**, so it is the most final thing they know how to do. The wallets go with them; so do the keys. *"It is the most secure custody solution the empire has ever devised,"* the parser notes. *"Nothing has ever been withdrawn."*

**The launch problem:** the ships have to get past **the verdlings**, who hold the open valley floor between the mountain and the water and poke anything that comes down off the Mingus — mourners included. So funerals are engineered around them:
- **The Sluice** — a long covered chute bored through the bottom of the range, ending at the river's edge. Verdlings are claustrophobic: they won't go near it. The ship goes down inside the mountain and out onto the water without ever touching the Runout. (When the Sluice jams, there are no funerals, and the empire's dead pile up in cold storage at the summit. The joke writes itself; let it.)
- **Night launches** at the old landing, timed for when the verdlings are asleep in the open — risky, fast, and the subject of several river-poet songs.

**Why it matters for the series:** a funeral ship is the only thing anyone has ever sent down the Verdance on purpose. In **Mingus II**, it's how the player gets onto the river — on a funeral ship the verdlings make ready for them at the end of Mingus I, launched through the Sluice. The gold on board is everyone's fortune and nobody's: the player can't spend a dead man's $ALT without his seed phrase, and the plaque on the mast is right there.

**The reservoir** *(a rumour in Mingus I; a place in a later game)*. Mingans whisper that somewhere on the old side there is a **reservoir as big as a city** — the old side's reservoir, the one that once held *all the water*. It's **empty now**. Nobody on the Mingus side has seen it; the river poets have a song about it that most people can't finish singing.

When the player finally reaches it (Mingus II from the far bank, or Mingus III on foot — author to decide), it should play exactly like this:
- **From the road it is nothing.** A long, low, grass-covered hill behind an old fence. A gate that hasn't opened in years. You could walk past it a hundred times.
- **Through the fence, up the grass.** The slope is gentle; the top is just a ridge of turf. You expect another field.
- **Then you look over the edge.** The ground drops away into a bowl so enormous the far side is a grey line. It is lined, all the way down, with **black concentric rings** — terraces, or tide marks, or courses of stone — narrowing toward a centre you can't quite focus on. **It is completely empty.** The scale is wrong for anything a person could have built. It does not look like a reservoir. It looks like the world has a hole in it and someone tidied the edges.
- **The feeling is vertigo and dread, not danger.** Nothing attacks. The player is told, plainly, that they feel dizzy and afraid, that their hands want to hold the grass — and the game lets them stand there as long as they like. `LOOK DOWN` should be one of the best room descriptions in the series. `JUMP` should be refused gently ("You don't. Nobody would.").
- **No explanation.** Why it was built, why it's empty, where all the water went — never answered. (The river poets think the Verdance is what's left of it. They might be right.)

**The reservoir as the goal — the draining of the Verdance** *(the trilogy's climax; author to place it, most naturally late in Mingus III)*. The river poets were half right: **the Verdance and the reservoir share one water.** Beneath the old side runs a great **source** — springs, an underground river, something that has never once stopped. **The headgate decides where it goes.** For generations it has been set to send the source out into the valley: that outpouring *is* the Verdance, and that is why the reservoir stands empty. Nobody remembers who chose to give the valley its river instead of keeping it in the bowl — only that someone, long ago, did.
- **The headgate.** At the reservoir's lip, half-buried in the grass, a squat stone house holds **one enormous, ancient wheel**, older than anything on the Mingus side, its single pointer set to *RIVER*. No buttons, no panel, no tools to find — just a wheel too big for one person, and the puzzle of how to move it (the builder's to design: counterweights, the current itself, help from someone on the old side).
- **Turning it back.** The wheel groans round to *RESERVOIR*. Nothing happens. Then, very slowly, the sound of the valley changes: the source has been turned home, and the Verdance — no longer fed — begins to run out.
- **The Verdance drains.** It takes all night; nobody on either bank sleeps. The river drops past the half-bridges, and from the reservoir's rim the great black rings far below begin, ring by ring, to fill — a city-sized bowl taking its water home. Across the valley **the verdlings are silent**: lined up along the Mingus bank, pikes lowered, watching the river they have guarded all their lives go quiet and thin. *They are terrified.*
- **And then the river is a riverbed.** Mud and stone and debris: the wrecks of old funeral ships still heaped with gold nobody can spend; the fallen ends of every half-bridge; drowned trees; the shiny glint of something on the far side of where the water was. **The Verdance is crossable.** For the first time in anyone's memory, there is nothing between the mountain and the old side but a walk.
- **On the old side, figures come down to the bank** — slowly, from the mine heads and the half-buried doors — and stand at the edge, and look at the empty bed where the river used to be. They don't speak. Nobody on either side moves first.
- **What it means** (never stated): the barrier was never the river; the river was a choice someone made — to give the valley its water — and the player has unmade it. Whether that was a good idea is left — like the lantern's meaning — to the river poets.

*Design note (the author's) — where the dread comes from:* the half-bridges and the giant reservoir come from the author's own dreams. His single lifelong recurring dream is of a **broken bridge he is forced to drive down — one that heads *into* the Mississippi instead of across it to the other side.** The reservoirs are dream images too. Builders: these scenes are meant to be **creepy as hell**, and they work by dream logic — the inevitability, the wrongness of scale, the bridge that goes into the water instead of over it. Never explain them away; never make them jokes. The satire lives in Mingus; the dread lives at the water.

*Design note (the author's):* this comes from life — Reservoir Park in Quincy, Illinois. At ten, the author and a friend went through the fence and climbed the grassy rise to peer over the edge of the reservoir: nothing like it looked from the street, enormous, empty, terrifying. The scene should carry that exact feeling — the ordinary hill, the transgression of the fence, and the moment the ground isn't there.

**The river poets.** The old side exists in Mingus culture mainly as **song**. Lyric poets — buskers on the gondola platforms, a tenor who plays the ballroom on Thursdays, a girl with a dulcimer at the bottom station — sing about it constantly. **None of them has ever seen it.** The songs are beautiful, specific, and contradict each other: in one the old side is all lamplight and low ceilings, in another it's an endless green field over a sleeping city, in another the river *is* the old side and the far bank is only its shore. Mingus officially disapproves (the songs are "bearish") and quietly streams them at 3 a.m. Vaunt has tried to buy the catalogue. Nobody will sell.

> *"Where the green bank goes down to the water,*
> *there's a door that was never a door —*
> *and the ones who went over the Verdance*
> *sing it better than we did before."*
> — busker, Gondola Platform 4

> *"Don't look for their lights from the ridgeline,*
> *they don't burn them to show you the way;*
> *they keep all their stars underneath them,*
> *and they've never once wished it was day."*
> — the Thursday tenor

> *"I floated a letter to the old side.*
> *It is floating there still, I suppose."*
> — dulcimer, bottom station (the whole song; she plays it twice)

**In play:** songs are an honest hint channel in a dishonest empire. A verse can point at something the player needs (in Mingus I, a song about "the one who looked down and saw where it fell" is a nudge toward the observatory telescope before the Runout finale); in Mingus II and III, the player finally sees parts of what the songs describe — and finds out which songs were right.

| | The Mingus Empire (up here) | The old side |
|---|---|---|
| **Brags about** | Sky, views, sunrise, "we have WEATHER," "elevated returns," light as an asset | Nothing. It does not brag. This is the problem. |
| **Actually has** | Wind, rockfalls, thin air, altitude sickness, freezing nights, lightning | Shelter, a steady temperature, quiet, the dark |
| **Visibility** | Everything is visible from everywhere. Nothing stays hidden. | Invisible. A green bank, a few chimney vents, a warm glow at dusk. |
| **Liabilities** | Roll downhill | Are, by geography, the Verdance's problem |

The old side is felt only as:

- **The glow on the far bank.** From the ridgeline overlooks you can see a few low chimney vents in the green, giving off a steady, warm, unremarkable light at dusk. It never flickers. The Mingus Empire's lights flicker constantly, and on stormy nights get struck by lightning. Nobody comments on this, loudly.
- **The telescopes.** The Observatory has twelve telescopes. All twelve point down at the far bank. None of them has ever seen anything.
- **Copied signage.** Many Mingus signs were obviously copied from somewhere else, then enlarged and hung upside down, because the originals were "for a basement."
- **Poached decor.** Chandeliers, doorknobs and one entire spiral staircase were "acquired." Staff become vague when asked how. The staircase still goes down. It is now bolted to a cliff and goes up.
- **Lies about fundamentals.** Every Mingus NPC says the old side is "basically closed," "underwater, financially," or "flooding as we speak." It is not.
- **The Verdance.** It marks the eastern edge of every map, and every Mingus map draws it narrower than it is. The far bank is green: real grass, real cottonwoods, real water. The Mingus side is red dust, scrub and spray-painted astroturf, and it resents this deeply.
- **"No one crosses the Verdance."** Every Mingus resident says it, half as a warning and half as a sales pitch. The whispered stories of explorers who crossed and never came back are the closest thing the empire has to a founding myth, and of course it has monetized them. There was a "Verdance Expedition" NFT drop (defunct; the roadmap ends at "Step 3: cross"). There is a bronze Lost Expedition plaque on the ridgeline overlook with a DONATE QR code where the names should be. And there is always someone whose cousin made it across.
- **Mrs. Thrumble.** The one person from the old side the player ever meets: its Clerk of Lost Property. She appears at a barred window on a far-side pier in the Verdance (Act I), at the bottom of the deepest mine shaft (Act II), and, under protest, by speakerphone in court (Act III). She is polite, efficient and kind. She does not acknowledge that the Mingus Empire exists. She has never once looked up.

**Sample lines**

> **Gondola Attendant**: "Welcome to the Mingus Empire. Four thousand feet higher than the competition. Well. Four thousand feet higher than the *announced* competition."

> **Murkling foreman**: "Down there they sit in the dark all day. The dark! Like potatoes. Up here we've got *weather*." (Lightning strikes the ridge. The lights go out.) "...That's weather."

> **Tour Guide**: "Darkness is a legacy technology. The Mingus Empire runs on light, which is why light costs 0.002 ETH per room."

> **Gift Shop Clerk**: "Don't buy the postcards from across there. They're smaller. Ours are the same picture, but bigger. It's grass."

> **Tollwarden Krugg**: "They charge nothing to get around across there. Nothing! That's how you know it's a scam."

> **Overlook Docent** (at the Lost Expedition plaque): "No one crosses the Verdance. Many have tried. Their sacrifice is commemorated here. Scan to honor them. Minimum honor is 0.01 ETH."

> **Murkling at the Gas Station**: "My cousin Dribb made it across. Swam it, middle of the night. Sent one message: 'it's warm here.' Then he just went quiet." (Beat.) "Hasn't posted since. Not once. Can you imagine?"

> **Lord Vaunt (recorded message)**: "People say to me, Maximus, is it true the old side is better? And I say: which old side? I've never heard of them. I hear they're closing. I hear it was never very good on the old side. Tremendous moisture problems. Up here we have sunrise. Every single day. Who else can say that?"

> **Mrs. Thrumble** (asked about the Mingus Empire): "I'm sorry, I don't know what you're referring to."

> **Mrs. Thrumble** (asked about the enormous gilded palace on the mountain): "I wouldn't know, dear. I don't look up."

> **Mrs. Thrumble** (shown an $ALT coin): "That appears to be a washer. Would you like to file it as lost?"

> **Parser** (if the player types `GO EAST` at the Verdance): "The Verdance is in the way. Beyond it, and below it, is a place you are not in, which the Mingus Empire would like you to know is worse."

**The rule for writers**: the old side gets no name, no rooms, no art, and no lines except Mrs. Thrumble's. Every reference to it comes from someone on this side, and every one of those people is a little insecure about it. Mingus brags about the sky and envies the dark. That is the whole relationship.

## THE COINS

### $ALT (Lord Vaunt's Token)

**Whitepaper excerpt**: *"$ALT is the first altitude-backed stablecoin, pegged to the sunrise itself. Unlike fiat currencies tied to outdated concepts like 'productivity' or 'trust,' $ALT derives value from the one constant in the Mingus Empire: it is very high up. Darkness is a legacy technology. Light is an asset class. Our sunrise is earlier than the competition's, by approximately four minutes. Elevated returns!"*

- **Logo**: Vaunt's face, gilded, with laser eyes, rising over a peak
- **Current status**: Down 94% from ATH ("all-time high," which in the Mingus Empire is also a place you can visit)
- **Holder benefits**: Access to Vaunt-controlled areas, reduced tolls, gondola priority boarding, "alpha" Discord channel (read-only)
- **Tagline**: *"Elevated returns!"*

### $SHRUG (Lady Vaunt's Token)

**Whitepaper excerpt**: *"Whatever. $SHRUG is for people who understand that value is what you decide it is. The coat collection is art. The tokenomics are math. It is cold up here, and the rest is your problem. DYOR."*

- **Logo**: A coat silhouette, cycling through colorways
- **Current status**: Surprisingly solvent (she hired an actual dev)
- **Holder benefits**: Access to Lady Vaunt's abandoned cliff wing, immunity from certain rug pulls, coat NFT airdrops
- **Tagline**: *"Whatever. DYOR."*

**Faction choice** affects NPC interactions, puzzle solutions, and ending variants. You can hold both coins, but key moments force a spend that reveals loyalty.

**Note**: Lady Vaunt is the only Mingus resident who never mentions the old side. Asked about it, she says: "Why would I care what's under a riverbank? Nobody on the old side is thinking about Max." This is the most threatening thing anyone says in the game.

## ACT I: THE ABANDONED PAVILION
*"You are standing on a wind-scoured mesa west of a pavilion that has seen better days. Better days, tremendous days, the best days—all behind it now. Far below to the east, past the Verdance, the far bank is green and quiet. It is not your concern. The wind would like to discuss your hat."*

### VAUNT PAVILION

The summit estate and front door of the Mingus Empire, perched on the highest mesa in the range so it can look down on the valley and on the far bank in particular. Maximus Vaunt is obsessed with the famous halls of the old side. He has never been inside them; he was politely asked to leave a ticket queue once, in 2019. He heard they were "deep," so he made his tall. He heard they were "carved," so he had his gilded. He heard they were "underground," and decided that was a branding problem.

The result is a sprawling estate that doesn't follow the golden ratio, or any ratio. Wings jut over the cliff edge at wrong angles. Doorframes are 12 feet tall for no reason. The doorbell is marble. Gilding peels in the wind. Foreclosure notices, weathered but persistent, flutter on every door, and every one that blows off rolls downhill, along with every other liability the estate ever had.

Most of it is abandoned now. But the service gondola still runs, and the mesa's root cellar still connects to the mine shafts inside the mountain. That root cellar is the only part of the estate Vaunt never shows guests and the only part he secretly likes.

### THE ABANDONED BALLROOM (Central Hub & Key Puzzle)

*"You enter a ballroom the size of a cathedral, if cathedrals were designed by someone who'd only heard of them secondhand. It is a replica of a great hall in the old side, built upside down. Gilded stalagmites hang from the ceiling. Gilded stalactites stand on the floor and trip you. The 'cave mouth' is a skylight. Where the original would have had a hundred feet of rock overhead, this has a hundred feet of sky, and the sky is leaking. A brass plaque insists the room is BIGGER, by 'about a foot, if you measure diagonally.' Now it is quiet, and the chandeliers—acquired, provenance unclear, designed to hang in a cellar—sway in a gale that very much should exist, because you are on a mountain."*

**Description**: The ballroom imitates the famous hall below, but it was built by someone working from rumors, a blurry postcard and ego instead of blueprints. Because the original is underground, Vaunt inverted everything to make it "elevated." Columns are thicker than necessary. The dance floor is a map of the Mingus Empire inlaid in gold, and its geography is wrong: the locations have been moved so they spell "VAUNT" when viewed from the mezzanine. The Verdance along the eastern edge is inlaid as a hairline crack, and the far bank is labeled simply "DAMP."

**Puzzle Function**: The ballroom floor IS a map, and a puzzle. Tiles can be depressed in sequence. The correct sequence—matching the empire's *real* geography, not Vaunt's vanity version—opens the hatch to the root cellar and the service lift into the mountain.

**Complication**: Storm clouds have parked over the skylight, so the floor is dark and only certain tiles are lit. To light more tiles, you must provide liquidity to the chandelier's smart contract. (Yes. The chandelier is on-chain. The one it was copied from runs on a wick.) Each tile costs gas. Lighting every tile at once is prohibitively expensive. The puzzle is finding the efficient path.

**Seed Word Location**: One word of the seed phrase is etched under the central tile, visible only when all adjacent tiles are lit. WORD 1 OF 12: **"HIGHER"**

### CRITICAL PATH

**The Whitepaper Switchbacks** *(Mandatory — Replaces the Grand Staircase)*

Before reaching the ballroom, you must climb the **Whitepaper Switchbacks**: a zigzag cliff stair lined with kiosks, each displaying a page of Vaunt's $ALT whitepaper. Every landing is blocked by a keyword lock.

**Sample Kiosk**:
> *"$ALT leverages decentralized synergy to create trustless value-capture mechanisms across the elevation-agnostic vertical."*

**Solution**: Translate the buzzwords into plain English and speak the translation to unlock.
- "Decentralized synergy" = "nobody's in charge"
- "Trustless value-capture" = "we take your money"
- "Elevation-agnostic vertical" = "a mountain"

Correct translation unlocks the landing: *"NOBODY'S IN CHARGE, WE TAKE YOUR MONEY, ON A MOUNTAIN."*

Parser response: "The kiosk beeps approvingly. It has never heard its own whitepaper stated so clearly. Somewhere, a VC weeps. The tears freeze."

**Bonus kiosk**: One kiosk reads *"Unlike legacy subterranean competitors, $ALT is built for the future."* Translation: *"THERE IS A PLACE UNDER THE FAR BANK AND IT IS DOING FINE."* The kiosk shuts itself off rather than display this.

---

**The KYC Forge** *(Mandatory — Bureaucracy Gate)*

To ride the service lift into the mountain, you need a **Verified Wallet Badge**: the on-chain version of **Form 77-Ω/b (Schedule: Whatever)**. The KYC Forge is a windswept terrace of terminals, each demanding more invasive verification.

**Requirements**:
- **Selfie with Newspaper** (today's date) — Find newspaper in abandoned study. It's from 2021. Parser: "Close enough for government work. Close enough for crypto."
- **Proof of Address** — Any foreclosure notice works. There are dozens, and more blow past every few moves.
- **CAPTCHA Completion** — A grid of images. "Select all squares containing murklings." Every image was taken inside the mine shafts. They are pitch black. All squares might contain murklings. This is the joke and also the puzzle. (Solution: select none. Murklings are invisible in the dark. You cannot prove they exist. Absence of evidence is evidence of absence, legally speaking.)

**Output**: **Verified Wallet Badge** (soulbound, non-transferable, can be revoked at any time for any reason).

Parser, on completion: "Verification complete. The Forge informs you that this form was adapted from a much shorter one used on the old side, and improved by adding pages and altitude."

### OPTIONAL THREADS

**The Gas Station** *(Economic Hub)*

A literal gas station on the gondola terrace. Pumps labeled "PREMIUM," "REGULAR," "MAINNET" and, at this altitude, "OXYGEN." Here you convert **shillets** to gas (ETH) and back. Shillets are the old, boring, reliable coin minted by the old side. Officially the Mingus Empire does not accept them. In practice nobody accepts anything else. The exchange rate is displayed on a ticker:

> CURRENT GAS RATE: 1 ETH = 47 SHILLETS
> (Updated every 10 moves. May vary. Will vary. Has varied.)
> SHILLETS ACCEPTED AS A COURTESY. WE DO NOT RECOGNIZE THEIR ISSUER.

**Mechanic**: Every action in the game costs gas. Simple actions (LOOK, INVENTORY) are free. Movement costs 0.001 ETH, doubled on switchbacks. Complex actions (puzzles, combat) cost more. Buying items with shillets avoids gas; buying with ETH triggers volatility.

Attendant: "Shillets, sure. They're from... a regional bank. Very small. Very old. In a basement. Probably flooding."

**Seed Word Location**: Carved into the underside of the pump handle. WORD 2 OF 12: **"BETTER"**

**The Verdance Grate** *(Optional — Lost Property)*

At the eastern edge of the mesa, a long switchback trail drops down the cliff, past the bronze Lost Expedition plaque (DONATE QR code; no names), to the valley floor. There the Verdance runs wide, cold and green. ### TOKEN A: Debugged Smart Contract *(Syntax Error Puzzle)*

function rugPull() public {

**Solution**: Add termination condition. `require(balance[holders[i]] > 0, "ALREADY RUGGED");`

### THE BELTED VERDLING *(REQUIRED — Act II or III; the key to the ending)*

High on the mountain, where no verdling should ever be, lives one anyway. **He left the valley years ago** — nobody, including him, will say why — and he has been **in hiding** ever since. *(The reveal, saved for the finale: he was the verdlings' chief. He walked away from all of them. The player is never told this in words.)* Being a verdling, he can't bear to go indoors (the mountain is all tunnels, galleries and "holes"), so he lives in the empire's open, windy, forgotten places: a rooftop above the Mint, the lip of an unused gondola platform, a terrace nobody has swept since the crash. He still has his pike. He is very polite, and very uncomfortable.

**His problem:** his **pants**. They are, he will tell you at length, *the finest pants a verdling can buy* — they cost a literal fortune — and they are held up by one of those **metal click belts**: pull it tight and it ratchets, *click, click, click*. Except the release is broken. He cannot undo the belt. He cannot slide out of the pants. And he will not cut them, because they are the best pants in the world. So for years he has been walking around the mountain, eating Mingan food, **gaining a little weight**, and feeling the pants and the belt slowly, patiently strangle his middle. He would be **so grateful** for any help.

> *The verdling shifts his weight, winces, and gestures at the buckle with exquisite dignity. "You'll notice," he says, "that I am not cutting them."*

**The puzzle** *(builder's design; suggested shape)*: the ratchet's release pawl is jammed — rusted shut and too small for fingers. Two things are needed:
1. **Something to free it** — oil or grease (from the Gas Station's pumps; the MAINNET pump leaks the good stuff).
2. **Something thin and stiff enough to press the pawl** — the funniest answer in Mingus: the tiny **SIM-eject pin** that comes in the box with every hardware wallet, which nobody has ever used for anything. (Alternatives that fail, in character: the pike — too big; a seed-phrase plaque — "No. Absolutely not. That's someone's whole life.")
Do it in the right order (`OIL BUCKLE`, then `PRESS PAWL WITH PIN`) and the belt lets go with one long, *glorious* exhale — from the verdling, not the belt.

**The reward — the only way to finish the game: his pike.** Overwhelmed, he gives it to you. *"Take it. I won't need it up here."* **You cannot complete Mingus I without it.** A verdling's pike is the one thing every verdling recognises and respects: on the Runout, **holding the pike lets you fend the verdlings off** (see the finale). Without it, the valley floor is unwinnable — you will always be poked back up, or worse.
He also tells you — quietly, looking at the valley he can't go back to — the one true thing about verdlings: *they're not guarding the valley from you. They're guarding you from the river.*

**Fairness rules for a required item** (builders):
- **Signpost it early.** A river-poet verse ("…and only a pike turns a pike aside…"), a faded warning sign at the bottom gondola station ("VERDLINGS RESPECT ONLY THE PIKE"), and the belted verdling himself leaning on his — the player should *know* a pike matters before they need one.
- **It can't be lost for good.** It can't be dropped in the river, sold, traded, burned, or minted (the Mint refuses: *"The machine declines. Some things are not assets."*); if put down, it stays where it was put.
- **Failure explains itself.** Every time the player is driven off the Runout without the pike, the text points at the missing piece: *"They were never going to let you past with your bare hands. Down here, only a pike gets a verdling's respect."*

## FINALE: THE DESCENT TO THE RUNOUT

*Mingus I is the fight down and out of the mountain. Whatever happened at the summit — a forked chain, a burned one, or Vaunt's helicopter wobbling off into the crosswind — the empire is finished, and the only way out is down. The game ends at the bottom: on the valley floor, at the edge of the Verdance, with the verdlings. It is the last puzzle in Mingus I and the first taste of Mingus II.*

**The descent** *(short, the run-out of the story)*: the gondolas are stalled, so it's the switchback stairs, the scree chutes and the emergency cable car with one working brake. A few last scenes, collapsing in reverse order of how the empire was built: the mint, the gas station, the ballroom's upside-down chandelier finally falling the right way up. Then the slope flattens, the wind drops, and you are standing on everything the empire ever lost.

### THE RUNOUT *(the finale — the Valley Floor)*

*"Everything in the Mingus Empire rolls downhill eventually. This is where it stops."*

At the base of the range, on the debris fan just before the Verdance, lies **the Runout**: eighteen months of everything the empire has dropped, knocked over, kicked off a terrace or let go of in a strong wind. Hard-wallet keychains. A gondola car. Several thrones. A crate of commemorative $ALT medallions, still shrink-wrapped. Lost seed-phrase notebooks, half of them still legible. A grand piano that got as far as the first switchback and then made its own decisions. Somewhere in the pile, almost certainly, a fortune.

Getting down is easy — gravity is the one thing in Mingus that has never once been down 94%. Staying is the problem.

**The Verdlings** *(the river's own people)*: little elves from the Verdance with a mean streak. Every one carries a pike. They do not want your treasure; they want you *gone* — back up the mountain, where Mingus people belong. They do not argue, negotiate, or accept tokens. They poke.

**And they are seriously dangerous.** The poking is a courtesy — the only one they extend. Mingus people joke about verdlings the way people joke about things that frighten them: the gondola staff won't run the bottom station after dusk; the Pavilion's insurance policy lists "verdling" as an act of God; nobody who has seen a verdling stop poking and start *meaning it* will describe what happens next. The whispered stories of explorers who crossed the Verdance and never came back are, everyone quietly agrees, verdling stories. They are small. They are patient. There are always more of them than you counted.

> *A verdling the height of your knee regards you with total contempt, then pokes you smartly in the shin with its pike. "Up," it says, pointing at the Mingus. "Up up up."*

**Mechanic — THE THREE-POKE RULE** *(the Runout's core puzzle)*:

Every turn you spend on the Runout costs one poke. Verdlings are precise about this. You get **three**.

1. **Poke one** — a sharp jab in the shin. *"Up."* The status line now shows **POKES: ●○○**.
2. **Poke two** — several verdlings, several jabs, harder. *"UP."* **POKES: ●●○**. Taking anything valuable from the pile costs a poke on its own (it is noticed).
3. **Poke three** — if you are still standing on the Runout when the third poke lands, you have run out of courtesy. *"The verdlings stop poking. That is the last thing you notice: how quiet it gets when they stop. Verdlings, it turns out, do not only poke."* **Death.**

**Leaving resets the count.** Going UP (or riding the gondola from the bottom station) before the third poke gets you out with a sore shin; the counter clears when you're off the Runout. So the valley is a place you **visit in short, planned trips** — the player learns the rule on the first trip (poke, poke, *run*) and then has to make two actions count.

**The finale puzzle — the pike is required.** The game ends when you reach **the river's edge** with the coat (and the seed word in its pocket). Walking toward the Verdance costs all three pokes at once — *without a pike, it is impossible*; every attempt ends with an escort uphill (or, if you push, the death).
- **Holding the belted verdling's pike, you can fend them off.** `FEND VERDLINGS WITH PIKE` (or simply carrying it raised) stops the pokes landing: the verdlings stop short, stare at the pike, and give ground — never far, never kindly. The poke count **freezes** while you hold the line, and the walk to the river's edge becomes possible.
- **But not unlimited.** Fending takes your attention: every turn you do something else (dig, take, climb), the pike is lowered and a poke can land. So the three-poke rule still governs *everything except* walking with the pike up — the player must still make their actions count.
- **Find the coat from above first.** The Pavilion's observatory telescope (Act I) shows where it landed — "by the upturned gondola car." The winning line on the Runout: `DIG BY GONDOLA` (poke one), `TAKE COAT` (poke two), then **raise the pike** (`FEND VERDLINGS WITH PIKE`) and walk **to the river's edge** with the pokes frozen. **The ending happens there** (see below).
- **Buy a turn by looking down on them** (one per trip) — still works, still funny, still not enough without the pike.

**The ending — the bowing.** At the river's edge, with the coat in one hand and the pike raised in the other, the player turns around. The verdlings have followed — not a few: the whole valley floor, from the Runout to the half-bridges, **thousands of them**, closer than they have ever come. The pokes stop. Then, one by one and then all at once, **they lower their pikes and bow.** They have recognised the pike. It belonged to the one who left — their chief, the verdling in the too-tight pants — and as far as they are concerned, whoever comes down off the mountain carrying it is **the one who comes next.**
The player is not triumphant. The prose should make them **stunned and deeply uneasy** — nobody asked for this, nobody explained it, the river is loud behind them and the valley is utterly silent in front. `BOW` gets a ripple of something like approval, which is worse. `WAIT` lets the silence go on.

**Then they part.** Any other action (or a few turns of waiting) and the verdlings **slowly part** — not scattering, *making way*, a lane opening down through the bowed crowd to the water's edge. At the end of the lane, moored at the Sluice, is a **ship like the funeral ships**: the same high prow, the same dressing of offerings and lanterns-that-aren't-lit — except this one has nobody laid out on it. It's for you. The verdlings have been standing guard at the launches for generations, and now they know who the next passenger is.

The player walks the lane (the only direction that works; every other direction, the crowd closes softly and bows again). **The game ends at the foot of the gangplank**, the player's hand on the rail, one step from going aboard:

> *Thousands of small heads stay bowed. The lane they made for you runs straight to the water, and the ship at the end of it rocks on the Verdance — the river nobody crosses and nobody comes back from. It was built to carry the dead. The gangplank is down. Nobody here is going to tell you to climb it, and nobody is going to let you go anywhere else.*
>
> *\*\*\* You have reached the bottom of the Mingus \*\*\**

— followed by the score and the Mingus II teaser ("Everything Rolls Downhill"). The player never boards in Mingus I; boarding is Mingus II's first move. *(Staging note: the mass bow is inspired by the shape of a famous film ending — the sudden submission and the hero's unease — but nothing from the film is used: no lines, no names, no imagery beyond "a people bows to a stranger who didn't ask for it.")*
**For Mingus II:** the game opens on the gangplank, with the player as the verdlings' reluctant chief. That's why — alone among Mingans — the verdlings let them go down to the water, and why the funeral ship is launched with verdlings standing guard instead of poking. Mingus II is the ride down the river.

**Variants the builder can choose:** after dark, the budget drops to **one** poke (and the gondola staff have gone home — the walk up is long); stepping toward the Verdance costs **all three** at once.

- **Verdlings never cross the Verdance. Ever.** The far side is too dangerous — the old side over there is *entirely underground*, and verdlings are **claustrophobic**. That is why they live where they do: the valley floor is the widest, most open, most sky-filled ground for miles, and they will not leave it. They don't go up the mountain either (too many tunnels, galleries and mine shafts — "holes," they call all of it, with disgust). Asked about the far bank, a verdling goes pale under its mud, looks at the river, looks at you, and pokes you — harder than usual. Stepping toward the Verdance costs all three pokes at once not out of malice but out of terror: they will not let you go somewhere that closed-in, and they will not follow you to stop you.
- **Their claustrophobia is also their weakness.** Anything enclosed on the Runout — the inside of the upturned gondola car, a culvert pipe, a crate big enough to crawl into — is a place no verdling will put its head. Ducking inside pauses the poke count; they wait outside, muttering, pikes ready. *(Builder's option: an alternative to the throne trick — one free turn per trip, and you still have to come out.)*
- They cannot be bribed, out-shouted, or tokenized. ("You offer the verdling 50 $ALT. It pokes the medallion, then you.")

**More ways to work the Runout** *(builder's suggestions, on top of the three-poke rule)*:
- **Send it down to them.** Anything you drop from the Pavilion's terraces in Act I lands in the Runout. The verdlings poke *objects* uphill too — so an item you need to move from the summit to the valley can be "delivered" by letting it roll, then retrieved by luring a verdling to poke it back up to the gondola station. Logistics, Mingus-style.
- **The Murkling route** *(Union path)*: murklings have tunnels that open onto the Runout at night. The verdlings tolerate murklings — both of them live near the dark — and escort only *non-miners* back uphill. Arrive with a union endorsement and a hard hat and they let you dig.

**What's down there that matters**: the **twelfth seed-phrase notebook** someone dropped from the Cold Wallet gondola (an alternative to computing the last word from the Merkle Root); Lady Vaunt's *original* coat, which rolled off a balcony during the divorce (the real one casts a shadow — and here, it also has a verdling pike-hole in the sleeve); and a crate of shillets, the old side's sober little coins, which every Mingus merchant pretends not to accept and quietly accepts.

**Seed Word Location** *(optional duplicate)*: scratched into a fallen gondola placard — the same word the Merkle Root computes. Completionists can find it the honest way: by digging through other people's losses.

## DESIGN NOTES

1. **The Ballroom as Thesis**: Maximus Vaunt's upside-down replica of the hall below is the game's central image. It is hubris built on hearsay: a sky palace imitating a cellar, grandeur without proportion, an empire without legitimacy, a copy of a place its maker never saw and could never have reached by building upward. It should feel sad and absurd at the same time.

2. **The Rivalry as Spine**: Every satirical beat should be able to point downhill. The old side is the control group: sheltered, solvent, quiet, real, and indifferent to the competition, which is exactly what makes the Mingus Empire's insecurity funny. The mountain has everything the cellar lacks (sky, sunrise, views, weather) and markets all of it. The cellar has the one thing the mountain cannot buy, which is somewhere to hide. Never show the old side. Never name it. Mrs. Thrumble is the only window, and her window has bars.

3. **Mountain Problems as Mechanics**: The empire's real problems should keep surfacing as game systems, not just jokes: wind (scatters paperwork), rockfalls (Lost Property), thin air (OXYGEN at the pump; altitude-sickness death), freezing nights (Cold Wallet timer; the real coat matters), lightning (summit hazards), and gravity (all losses roll downhill, toward the Verdance and the people who don't look up).

4. **Dual Economies**: Shillets (stable, old-school, "not accepted," accepted everywhere) vs. ETH (volatile, new chaos). Players must manage both. Neither is "better." Shillets are reliable but limited; ETH is powerful but unpredictable. No Mingus NPC should ever point out that the empire runs on its rival's money.

5. **Faction Fluidity**: Holding both coins is allowed. The game only forces a choice at key moments. This mirrors real crypto tribalism: everyone claims loyalty until the trade is right.

6. **Seed Phrase as Meta-Puzzle**: The 12 words are scattered on purpose. Completionists will find them all; casual players will finish without them. The lamp is a nostalgic reward, not a mechanical one. It grants no power, only meaning: the one real light on the mountain was made for the dark.

7. **Satirical Targets**: Crypto bros (obvious), but also crypto skeptics (the "realistic ending" mocks smug I-told-you-so energy), reformists (the "clean" ending is still just another system), anarchists (the "chaos" ending solves nothing), and anyone who has ever defined themselves against a rival who isn't paying attention. Everyone gets roasted.

8. **Parser Voice**: Exhausted, dry, slightly pitying. It has processed millions of commands. It has seen every ending. It does not judge; it merely observes, with a sigh. It is the only voice in the Mingus Empire that mentions the old side neutrally.

9. **Content Note (bundled sample)**: The game is suitable for a public demo. It contains no profanity. Its political satire targets archetypes (the bombastic founder-heir, the indifferent spouse with the competent dev), not real people, and no real person's name appears anywhere in it. Mingus and the Verdance are fantasy names that borrow the flavor of a real mountain range and river; the places in the game are invented.

## THE MINGUS SERIES (arc)

The trilogy walks the player down the mountain, one game at a time — and gets closer to the thing nobody in Mingus will talk about.

| # | Title | Where | The premise |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | **Elevated Returns** *(this game)* | From the summit down to the valley floor | The crypto-gilded mountain empire at its peak, its crash — and the fight down and out of the mountain. Ends on the Runout: the three-poke verdling finale, the bowing, and the verdlings parting to reveal a funeral ship — the game ends at its gangplank. |
| II | **Everything Rolls Downhill** | **Down the Verdance**, alternating banks | "No one crosses the Verdance." Nobody said anything about *following* it. You get onto the river the only way anyone ever has — on a **funeral ship**, through the Sluice — and ride it downstream, putting in on the **Mingus bank** (open river-land, verdling country), out on the **water**, and — taboo broken — on the **old-side bank**, where the old side's mines and half-buried buildings begin. The river is enormous, a world of its own. Rivers roll downhill too. |
| III | *(title TBD)* | **Stranded on the old-side bank, then the forest** | Your boat is wrecked on the far side. The only way home is a long trek back up the old-side bank — past mine heads, shafts and underground buildings you never enter and nobody names — and then through the forest. **The trilogy's last scene:** the trees open onto a clearing, and in the clearing stands a house. It's a small house. It's white. *End of Mingus III, and of the trilogy.* |

**Mingus II — the tough one (notes, not yet designed):** it isn't all on the water. It **switches between banks**: the **Mingus bank** (river-land on the mountain side) → **the Verdance itself** → the **old-side bank** (river-land on the old side) → and back. The old-side bank is where the far side stops being rumour: **mines and lots of underground buildings** — entrances, shafts, half-buried halls — seen from outside and at the edges, never fully entered or explained, and **never named**. Nothing on that bank is identified as belonging to any existing game or empire; it is simply *the old side*, built down instead of up. Crossing at all breaks "No one crosses the Verdance" — that taboo is the game's tension. The verdlings stay on the Mingus bank's open ground (they won't cross, and they won't go near anything underground), so the old-side bank has its own, quieter dangers.

**Mingus III (notes):** the boat is wrecked and you are stranded on the old-side bank. The game is the walk home — upriver along the old side's edge, then inland through the forest. The old side is still never entered, explained or named; it is landscape, not a destination.

**The final image (handle with restraint):** the white house is a single, unnamed closing picture — a wink for those who know, never a crossover. No mailbox, no boarded door, no leaflet, no named room, no description beyond the clearing and the house; the game ends on the first sight of it.

**Series rules:** the old empire stays unnamed and unseen in all three (at most, Mingus III sees its *edge* from the water); verdlings never cross the river and never go underground, so in Mingus III they are a shoreline threat, not a passenger; each game stands alone and ends with a tease of the next.
